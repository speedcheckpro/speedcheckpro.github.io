Download vs Upload Speed

SpeedCheck Pro Guide • Updated November 2026 • 5 min read

Every speed test shows two numbers, download and upload, and they are not the same thing. Download speed is how fast data travels from the internet to your device. Upload speed is how fast data travels from your device to the internet. You use both all day, often without noticing, but they matter for different activities. Understanding the difference helps you pick the right plan and work out why a connection can feel fast for one thing and slow for another.

Download speed explained

Download speed controls how quickly things arrive on your device. When you open a web page, watch a video, download an app, or receive a file, you are downloading. A higher download speed means pages load faster, videos start sooner and play without buffering, and large files finish sooner. Most of what people do online is downloading, which is why providers advertise the download number in large print and why speed tests put it first. If your household mainly watches video, browses, and downloads, download speed is the number that matters most to you.

Upload speed explained

Upload speed controls how quickly data leaves your device. When you send an email with an attachment, back up photos to the cloud, post a video, or speak on a video call, you are uploading. It is the number that decides whether the people on your video call see you clearly or as a frozen, pixelated picture. Upload speed also matters for online gaming, because your console or computer constantly sends your actions to the game server, and for live streaming, where your video is uploaded in real time. If you work from home, upload speed may matter more to you than download speed. Our guide to internet speed for video calls explains the numbers in detail.

Why download is usually faster than upload

On most home connections, download speed is much higher than upload speed. A plan sold as 100 Mbps might give you 100 Mbps down and only 10 or 20 Mbps up. This is deliberate: providers know that most households download far more than they upload, so they give the download direction most of the capacity. Cable and DSL connections are built this way, with the two directions sharing a fixed amount of capacity. Fibre connections are different: many fibre plans offer the same speed in both directions, which is one reason fibre feels so good for video calls and cloud backups. If your upload speed is holding you back, fibre is worth asking your provider about.

How much of each do you need?

The right numbers depend on what you do online. Use the table as a rough guide for a single person doing one thing at a time, then multiply for a busy household.

ActivityDownload neededUpload needed
Browsing and email1 to 5 Mbps1 Mbps
Video streaming in HD5 to 8 MbpsLess than 1 Mbps
Video calls3 to 8 Mbps3 to 8 Mbps
Online gaming5 to 25 Mbps3 to 5 Mbps
Cloud backup and large uploads5 Mbps10 Mbps or more

Notice that video calls are the one common activity where upload matters as much as download. A household with two people on video calls at once needs at least 10 to 15 Mbps of upload to keep both calls smooth. If your plan only gives you 5 Mbps up, that is where the freezing starts. How to test both speeds

Run a speed test and look at both numbers, not just the big download figure. Test on a wired connection if you can, because Wi-Fi can hide the true speeds your plan delivers. Test at different times of day, since upload and download can both dip in the evening. If your download is close to what you pay for but your upload is far below it, check whether your provider offers a plan with a faster upload, or whether a fibre connection is available at your address. Also remember that ping affects how responsive your connection feels, and it is reported separately from both speeds.

Tip: If your video calls freeze but streaming works fine, suspect your upload speed first. Close cloud backup apps and photo syncing during calls, and test again — those apps quietly use your upload in the background.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my upload speed so much slower than my download speed?

Most home connections are built with more capacity for downloading than uploading, because households usually download far more than they upload. Cable and DSL plans are often asymmetric by design. Fibre plans are the main exception, and many offer equal speeds in both directions.

How much upload speed do I need for video calls?

A single high-definition video call needs about 3 to 8 Mbps of upload. Two people on calls at once need roughly double that. If your upload is below 5 Mbps and you work from home, a plan with a faster upload will make a real difference.

Does upload speed affect online gaming?

Yes, but only a little. Gaming needs about 3 to 5 Mbps of upload to send your actions to the server. What matters far more for gaming is ping: a low, stable ping makes the game feel responsive, while a high ping causes lag even on a fast connection.
