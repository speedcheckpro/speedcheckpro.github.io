Does a VPN Affect Internet Speed? The Honest Answer

SpeedCheck Pro Guide • Updated November 2026 • 5 min read

If you use a VPN, you have probably noticed it: the speed test that once read 200 Mbps now reads 150, and pages take a fraction longer to load. That slowdown is real, and it is the price of what a VPN does. A VPN encrypts your traffic and sends it through an extra server before it reaches the website you want. Both steps take time. The question is not whether a VPN slows you down, but by how much, and whether the trade is worth it for you.

Why a VPN Slows Your Connection

A VPN adds two extra jobs to every piece of data you send. First, encryption: your device scrambles your traffic so nobody on the network can read it, and the VPN server unscrambles it at the other end. Modern encryption is fast, but it still costs a few milliseconds. Second, the detour: instead of travelling straight to the website, your data goes to the VPN server first, and that server may be in another city or another country. The longer the detour, the bigger the delay. Together, these steps usually cut your speed by 10 to 20 percent, and add 10 to 30 ms to your ping.

How Much Speed Will You Actually Lose?

The drop depends on three things: your starting speed, the VPN server you choose, and the protocol the VPN uses. On a fast fiber connection, a good VPN might take you from 500 Mbps to 400 Mbps, a loss you will never notice in normal use. On a 25 Mbps connection, the same percentage drop hurts more, because every megabit matters. Server choice is the biggest lever. A server in your own city adds almost no distance, while one on another continent can halve your speed. If your VPN offers a choice of protocols, the newer WireGuard protocol is usually faster than the older OpenVPN. To measure the real difference on your own connection, follow our guide on how to test internet speed accurately and run the test with the VPN on and off.

When a VPN Can Actually Make Things Faster

It sounds strange, but a VPN can sometimes improve your speed. Some internet providers deliberately slow down certain kinds of traffic, such as video streaming, during busy hours. This is called throttling. Because a VPN hides what kind of traffic you are sending, your provider cannot single it out, and the throttling stops working. The same applies to bad routing: if your provider's route to a particular server is congested, a VPN can send your traffic along a cleaner path. These cases are the exception, not the rule, but they explain why some people report faster speeds with a VPN switched on.

Does a VPN Affect Gaming and Streaming?

For streaming, a VPN rarely causes problems. A 4K stream needs about 25 Mbps, and most connections have far more than that even after the VPN takes its share. Gaming is more sensitive. Games care about ping, not bandwidth, and the extra hop through a VPN server adds delay. Competitive players should turn the VPN off while they play, or choose a server as close as possible. Our guide on how to lower ping in games has more on keeping latency down. Video calls sit in the middle: a VPN is usually fine for a one-to-one call, but a weak connection can start to stutter.

Tip: Always connect to the VPN server nearest to you unless you need a specific country. The closest server gives you the smallest speed loss, and most VPN apps have an "auto" or "fastest server" option that picks it for you.

How to Keep Your VPN Fast

A few habits keep the slowdown small. Use a wired connection or sit close to your router, because a VPN cannot fix a weak Wi-Fi signal. Switch to the WireGuard protocol if your VPN supports it. Try a different server when speeds dip, since individual servers get crowded. Keep your VPN app updated, and avoid free VPNs: they often run overcrowded servers and sell your data, which defeats the purpose. If speed matters more than privacy for a particular task, use split tunneling, a feature in many VPN apps that lets you send only some apps through the VPN and the rest directly.

A VPN does slow your internet, but for most people the cost is small: a 10 to 20 percent drop and a few extra milliseconds of ping. On a decent connection you will not feel it. Choose a nearby server, use a modern protocol, and test your speed with the VPN on and off so you know exactly what you are giving up. If the drop is much bigger than 20 percent, the problem is usually the server you picked, not the VPN itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a VPN slow down internet speed?

Yes, in most cases. A VPN encrypts your traffic and routes it through an extra server, which typically cuts your speed by 10 to 20 percent and adds 10 to 30 ms of ping. The exact loss depends on the server you choose and the protocol your VPN uses.

Can a VPN increase internet speed?

Sometimes. If your internet provider throttles video streaming or other traffic, a VPN hides the type of traffic you are sending, so the throttling cannot target it. A VPN can also help if your provider's route to a server is congested. These cases are the exception rather than the rule.

Which VPN protocol is fastest?

WireGuard is generally the fastest widely available protocol, because it uses modern, lightweight encryption. OpenVPN is slower but very well tested, and IKEv2 sits in between. If your VPN app offers WireGuard, try it first and compare your speeds.
